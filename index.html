THE LIFECYCLE OF TECHNOLOGY

Where does your old technology go?

Every year, people replace millions of electronic devices. But what happens to the phones, computers, and gadgets we leave behind?

EXPLORE THE FACTS ↓
01 / THE BIGGER PICTURE

Technology doesn't simply disappear.

When electronics are replaced or thrown away, they become part of a growing waste problem. Some devices can be repaired or reused, while others are recycled or sent to landfills. Understanding what happens next can help us make better decisions about the technology we use.

What happens to electronics when we throw them away? +

Electronic devices that are thrown away become part of the growing global e-waste problem. Some are sent to formal recycling facilities, where valuable materials can be recovered. Others may end up in landfills or informal recycling sites, where they are not always handled safely.

According to the Global E-waste Monitor 2024, the world generated approximately 62 million metric tonnes of electronic waste in 2022. However, only 22.3% was documented as formally collected and recycled in an environmentally sound manner.

Why it matters: A large amount of electronic waste is still not being properly managed.

Is repairing or donating an old device better than replacing it? +

In many situations, repairing or reusing a device can be a better first step than immediately replacing it.

  • Repair: Replacing a battery or damaged screen may allow a device to remain useful for longer.
  • Donate or reuse: Giving away a working device allows someone else to continue using it.

Extending the life of electronics can help reduce the demand for new devices and slow the amount of waste being produced. However, not every device can be repaired or safely reused.

Why it matters: The best option depends on the device's condition, repair costs, and whether someone else can still benefit from it.

Why can electronic waste be harmful to people and the environment? +

Electronics can contain hazardous substances that may be harmful to people and the environment.

Some examples include:

  • Lead
  • Mercury
  • Cadmium

When devices are improperly dismantled, burned, or recycled, these substances can contaminate the surrounding air, soil, water, and food.

The World Health Organization (WHO) explains that people living or working near unsafe electronic waste recycling sites can be exposed to these harmful substances. Children are especially vulnerable because their bodies and nervous systems are still developing.

Why it matters: E-waste is not just an environmental issue; it is also a public health concern.

What should you do when you no longer need an electronic device? +

Before throwing away an electronic device, consider its condition and whether someone else could use it.

  • Repair: If the device has a fixable problem, repairing it can extend its lifespan.
  • Donate or reuse: If it still works, consider giving it to someone else or donating it to an organization that accepts electronics.
  • Recycle: If it can no longer be used or repaired, look for a certified electronics recycling program that can handle it safely.
  • Discard: Avoid putting electronics in regular household trash when proper electronic waste collection is available.

Remember: There is no single perfect choice for every device. The goal is to avoid unnecessary waste, keep usable electronics in circulation, and make sure electronics are handled responsibly.
